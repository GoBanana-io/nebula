## The problem

In 2010 the Arab shopper hunting discounted premium brands had nowhere trustworthy to click: no curated flash-sales club, no cash-on-delivery culture online, and delivery networks that treated a Riyadh order from Amman as an expedition.

## How it works

Founded in Jordan in November 2010 by CEO Ahmed Alkhatib (ex-Zazzle) and CTO Amer Abulaila, MarkaVIP adapted the Vente Privee/Gilt flash-sales model to the region — limited-time, members-only sales of premium apparel, lifestyle products, and electronics across the GCC, Jordan, and Lebanon. To fix delivery, it experimented with running its own couriers in Jordan and Lebanon and pushed warehouses and staff into Saudi Arabia, its biggest market.

## Pain points

Authenticity fears around discounted luxury, cash-on-delivery as the default payment rail, delivery lead times measured in weeks, and a Saudi market that demanded on-the-ground warehouses and personnel.

## Business model

Classic flash-sales retail: buy premium inventory, sell it fast at a discount, and live off repeat — at peak, a buyer who purchased once came back about once a month, with 5,000-plus new members joining daily.

## Challenges

The model never escaped the logistics trap: each new market meant new warehouses, couriers, and cash burn, while Souq (soon Amazon), Namshi, and the billion-dollar Noon project squeezed margins and raised customer-acquisition costs. The 2017 acquisition by China's JollyChic ended independence, and when JollyChic's own site and seller centre went dark in January 2022, the MarkaVIP brand went with it — a consolidation casualty, not a product failure.

In January 2022 Momentum Works reported that JollyChic — which Wamda confirmed had acquired Jordanian rival MarkaVIP in 2017 — had a site and seller centre that were no longer accessible, marking the end of the road for the MarkaVIP brand under its acquirer.
