## The problem

Most road deaths trace to seconds of human inattention — a missed brake light, a drift across the lane, a hazard spotted too late. Automakers knew cameras could help but had no affordable, car-grade vision system they could bolt onto ordinary family cars.

## How it works

An occupier-company headquartered in Jerusalem (Occupied Palestine), majority-owned by Intel — not a Palestinian company. Founded in 1999 by Prof. Amnon Shashua and Ziv Aviram, Mobileye built its business on a single forward camera, its EyeQ system-on-chip, and vision software that detects lanes, vehicles, and pedestrians in real time. Intel bought the company for $15.3B in 2017 after shareholders approved the sale, took it public again on Nasdaq in 2022 while keeping majority ownership, and Mobileye has since layered SuperVision (multi-camera hands-free driving) and Chauffeur (eyes-off autonomy) plus REM crowdsourced road maps on top of the EyeQ base.

## Pain points

Tightening crash-safety ratings pushing ADAS into every trim level; automakers without the AI teams to build vision stacks themselves; autonomy programs burning cash with production dates that keep slipping.

## Business model

Per-vehicle licensing at massive scale: EyeQ chips in the tens of dollars across hundreds of millions of cars, SuperVision systems near $2,000 per vehicle, Chauffeur licences higher still — locked in through multi-year OEM design wins, with mapping and data services on top.

## Challenges

Full self-driving arrived years late across the whole industry; Mobileye lost Tesla as a customer to in-house silicon; and the SuperVision/Chauffeur bets must survive automaker cost-cutting and prove safety regulators and buyers will pay autonomy premiums.
