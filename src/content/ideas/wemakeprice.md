## The problem

Korean e-commerce runs on 50–60-day seller settlement cycles: marketplaces hold merchants' money for weeks before paying out. That float is invisible in good times — and catastrophic when the operator runs dry, because thousands of small sellers discover at once that their revenue is trapped inside someone else's liquidity crisis.

## How it works

WeMakePrice was a discount-led open marketplace in the first wave of Korean social commerce: sellers listed goods, shoppers chased flash deals and coupons, and the platform settled with merchants about two months after each sale. Qoo10 acquired it in April 2023 (alongside TMON in August 2022) to build a roll-up headed for a Nasdaq IPO. When Qoo10's acquisition debt caught up with it, payouts to sellers froze in July 2024 and both platforms filed for court receivership on July 29, 2024.

## Pain points

Merchants waiting two months for money that never arrived; consumers unable to cancel card transactions after payment-gateway vendors pulled out; travel agencies and small sellers with no reserves, forcing the government to assemble 560 billion won in emergency liquidity and the financial supervisor to order gateways to restore cancellations.

## Business model

Commission and advertising on high-volume discounted sales — a thin-margin game that depended on ever-growing transaction flow, then subordinated to Qoo10's IPO-roll-up arithmetic instead of being fixed on its own terms.

## Why it worked (or didn't)

It didn't. The government counted 213.4 billion won in unsettled payments across the two platforms — 85.4 billion won of it owed to WeMakePrice merchants. Eighteen months of court-supervised rehabilitation produced no buyer (unlike sibling TMON, which Oasis picked up), and in September 2025 the court terminated proceedings, finding it "clearly established that the liquidation value of the debtor's business exceeds its going-concern value." On November 10, 2025, the Seoul Bankruptcy Court declared WeMakePrice bankrupt.

## Challenges

A parent company that bought growth with debt and pledged personal assets too late; a settlement model that concentrated risk on the smallest merchants; and a brand so damaged by the payout freeze that no acquirer would touch it even at distressed prices.
