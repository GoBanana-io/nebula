## The problem

Co-founder Abdelrazek Aly broke his hand as a Qatar University engineering student and, seeking help at the special-needs centre, met a blind law student waiting on volunteers to transcribe lectures into a format his $5,000 braille display could read. That three-step chain — professor to volunteer to expensive hardware — is how most blind students still get their education.

## How it works

Bonocle is a portable handheld with a braille cell that pairs with everyday smartphones over Bluetooth, translating digital content into touch on the spot. Around it sits a learning and entertainment library: apps for the braille alphabet, spelling, maths, and chemistry, plus board games, platformers, action-adventure, and puzzle titles — including work showcased for visually impaired fans during the World Cup.

## Pain points

Transcription delays that leave blind students weeks behind, hardware priced for institutions instead of people, and braille tools that feel like medical equipment rather than something a teenager would pick up.

## Business model

Affordable device sales to individuals — the first batches sold in Qatar — plus institutional deals with schools and accessibility centres, with the software library deepening the product over time.

## Challenges

Consumer hardware economics at small volumes, country-by-country deals with education systems, and the content treadmill of keeping learning apps and games engaging.
