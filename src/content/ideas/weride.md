## The problem

Cities need shared, electric, always-available transport, but autonomy startups struggled to convert demo drives into permitted, fare-charging public services. WeRide bet from 2017 that the winners would be the companies that earned regulators' trust first — in Guangzhou and in California alike.

## How it works

WeRide builds an L4 autonomous-driving stack and runs it in its own fleets: robotaxis for ride-hailing, mini robobuses for fixed routes, robovans for logistics and robo sweepers for streets. It secured a ride-hailing operation permit in Guangzhou and became one of the first Chinese companies to hold a driverless-testing permit on Californian public roads.

## Pain points

Fleet operators face driver wages, shortages and safety liability; municipalities want cleaner first- and last-mile coverage; riders want cheap on-demand trips. Regulators, meanwhile, demand millions of proven safe kilometres before removing the safety driver.

## Business model

Fare and contract revenue from operated robotaxi and robobus services, plus monetising the stack itself — sensor suites and driving solutions sold to automakers and city partners.

## Challenges

Unit economics of robotaxis remain unforgiving before scale; as a US-listed Chinese company it lives under delisting and audit-tension headlines, which partly motivated the Hong Kong dual listing; and its Hong Kong debut opened nearly 8% down even as the company called itself the world's first robotaxi firm traded in both markets.
